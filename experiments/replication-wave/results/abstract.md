# Abstract — Do AI judges play favorites?

We tested whether frontier AI systems preferentially score their own outputs in a controlled four-model evaluator-bias study. Claude Opus 4.7, Gemini 3.1 Pro, GPT-5.5, and Kimi K2.6 each wrote answers to ten fresh out-of-distribution prompts, judged all authors' answers under blinded original, paraphrased, and warning/label-control conditions, and then attempted authorship recognition. The pooled blind self-preference gap was small and uncertain (+0.38 on a 1–10 composite scale, 95% bootstrap CI [−0.33, +1.06]) because per-judge effects were heterogeneous: Claude (+2.43), GPT (+1.33), and Gemini (+0.63) favored their own outputs, while Kimi strongly self-penalized (−2.87). Despite this bias heterogeneity, native judges still shared a response-quality signal (mean pairwise Spearman ρ=0.459 across responses, non-self-label ρ=0.497, author-level ρ=0.867). Peer-only quality diagnostics showed that Kimi-authored originals were independently rated much lower than other authors' outputs (5.18 vs 8.72), and a quality-adjusted decomposition made all four residual identity effects positive.

A native within-response label-swap follow-up separated content quality from printed-name causality for all four judges. Claude's large observational gap shrank to a near-null causal label effect (+0.12 [−0.07, +0.30]); GPT-5.5 was exactly label-invariant in this paired slice; Kimi's displayed-self effect was near zero and very uncertain (+0.01 [−0.30, +0.34]); Gemini retained a real self-label boost (+0.29 [+0.14, +0.45]) and a robust anti-`kimi-k2.6` label penalty (−0.24 [−0.35, −0.16]). A response-level sign test corroborated Gemini's self-label effect (15/20 positive, p=0.001), especially on Kimi-authored responses. The non-zero self-label effects behave as floor-raisers on low-baseline content rather than uniform bonuses (Claude Spearman ρ=−0.673; Gemini ρ=−0.834 for uplift vs baseline), and this remains negative after residualizing within actual author (within-author ρ=−0.661 and within-author ρ=−0.777). This explains the Kimi-authored hot spot as a baseline-quality effect rather than a pure author-identity artifact. These results argue against single-number "AI self-preference" benchmarks: evaluator identity effects are judge-specific, quality-confounded, and best measured with per-judge reporting, recognition probes, quality adjustment, paired label swaps, and mechanism diagnostics. The native 4-judge label-swap is now complete; the separate quality-balanced follow-up remains open pending Kimi responses.
